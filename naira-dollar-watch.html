Naira-Dollar Watch: What Moved the Exchange Rate This Week

By WolStreet Finance Desk · July 24, 2026 · 5 min read

If you checked a rate app this week and did a double take, you weren't alone. The Naira moved against the dollar again, and as usual, the reasons are a mix of what the Central Bank did, what traders expect, and what's happening with oil and forex inflows. Here's the breakdown without the jargon.

What actually happened

The parallel market rate and the official window rate both shifted this week, narrowing the gap between them slightly. That gap matters more than either number alone — a wide spread between the "black market" rate and the official rate is usually a sign of scarcity, low confidence, or both. A narrowing gap generally means more dollars are flowing through official channels instead of side routes.

Why it moved

  • CBN interventions: Periodic dollar sales to bureaux de change and banks ease short-term pressure, though the effect tends to fade if it isn't backed by sustained inflows.
  • Oil receipts: Nigeria's forex earnings are still heavily tied to crude sales. Higher oil prices or output support the Naira; disruptions do the opposite.
  • Diaspora remittances: A growing share of forex now comes through formal remittance channels rather than informal ones, which the CBN has actively encouraged with rate reforms.
  • Interest rate policy: A high Monetary Policy Rate is partly designed to make Naira assets attractive enough to pull in foreign portfolio investment, which supports the currency.

What it means for you

If you're paid in Naira but spend in dollars — school fees abroad, subscriptions, imported stock for a business — a weaker Naira means budgeting a buffer, not panicking. If you hold savings, this is usually the moment people ask about dollarizing part of their portfolio through domiciliary accounts, dollar-denominated mutual funds, or eurobonds, each with different access and risk trade-offs worth understanding before you move money.

Worth remembering: Exchange rate swings are normal and expected in an economy still stabilizing its forex market. React to the trend over months, not the daily number.

We'll keep tracking this weekly — the goal isn't to predict where the rate lands next, it's to make sure you understand why it moves, so the next headline doesn't catch you off guard.
